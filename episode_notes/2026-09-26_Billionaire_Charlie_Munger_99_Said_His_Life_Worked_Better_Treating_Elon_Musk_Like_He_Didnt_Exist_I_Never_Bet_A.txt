The late longtime Berkshire Hathaway Vice Chair Charlie Munger didn't need to dislike a business to stay away from it. Sometimes, he simply decided it belonged in the "too hard" pile.

Tesla and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk was a perfect example. Munger respected what Musk had accomplished, but he wasn't interested in taking the same risks that helped get him there.

The two had a history that went back to Tesla's early days.

"I was at a lunch with Munger in 2009 where he told the whole table all the ways Tesla would fail," Musk wrote on X in 2022. "Made me quite sad, but I told him I agreed with all those reasons and that we would probably die, but it was worth trying anyway."

Tesla obviously didn't die. In fact, its success eventually forced Munger to acknowledge that Musk had accomplished something extraordinary.

At Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting in 2023, Munger said there hadn't been a successful new American auto company in a long time and called Tesla's achievement a "minor miracle." He also acknowledged that Musk had accomplished important things by pursuing problems that seemed almost impossible to solve.

That history made Munger's final interview even more interesting.

The CNBC interview was taped just two weeks before his death at 99. It was originally intended to air around what would have been his 100th birthday, but Munger died on Nov. 28, 2023.

When host Becky Quick brought up Musk, Munger made clear that he still wasn't convinced the Tesla CEO's fortune was as secure as it appeared.

"I don't regard Elon Musk as truly that rich because I don't think it's sure that everything he's working on can work," Munger said. "I think he can get his a** handed to him on autos. I would not invest in Elon Musk's autos myself."

Munger Respected Musk Without Wanting His Risk

Quick then pointed out that Munger had clearly been impressed by what Musk had built.

"Yes, well, who cannot?" he replied.

The answer wasn't a dismissal of Musk's accomplishments. Instead, Munger used the opening to explain what he believed was behind them.

"Again, that's another lesson," Munger said. "What is the lesson of Elon Musk? Somebody who has a perfectly ridiculous amount of money almost always has an element of luck in him.".

He then returned to Musk's willingness to keep doubling down on his biggest bets.

"He's been quite lucky in what he's picked to double down, double down, double down — and then he's used leverage so much that he has doubled down right to the edge of extinction maybe two or three times," Munger said.

That kind of risk-taking was precisely what Munger preferred to avoid.

"How many people can go to the edge of extinction without stepping into it?" he asked. "Well, he's done it three times. Maybe he's got six more in him — I don't know."

Then came the clearest explanation of where Musk fit into Munger's investment framework.

"I put Elon Musk in a too-hard pile," he said. "I never bet against him, but I never bet with him."

Munger finished with a line that captured just how completely he preferred to keep that kind of risk outside his own portfolio.

"As far as I'm concerned, he doesn't exist. My life works better if I treat the world as though he didn't exist," he said.

Compounding Was Munger's Other Big Bet

Munger's own approach to wealth building was almost the mirror image of Musk's willingness to repeatedly make enormous bets.

He and Warren Buffett built Berkshire around patience, discipline, rational decision-making and the power of compounding. Rather than constantly chasing the next huge opportunity, Munger emphasized finding good businesses, avoiding obvious mistakes and allowing time to work.

That philosophy didn't require investors to predict the next Tesla. It required them to stay invested long enough for compounding to matter.

Munger's record also showed why he placed such a high value on knowing the limits of one's own understanding. If an investment was too difficult to understand or carried risks that couldn't be comfortably assessed, walking away was an option.

Munger could recognize an extraordinary achievement without deciding that it belonged in his portfolio.

The Munger Lesson Still Applies At Any Age

The broader lesson wasn't that every investor should avoid ambitious companies or copy Munger's exact portfolio. It was that wealth building doesn't have to depend on finding one spectacular winner.

Munger spent a lifetime studying the mechanics of wealth. His final thoughts on Musk showed another part of that philosophy — knowing when an opportunity is impressive, knowing when it's risky and knowing when it's simply better left in the too-hard pile.
