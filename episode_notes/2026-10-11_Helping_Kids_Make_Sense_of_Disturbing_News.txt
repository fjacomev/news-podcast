Growing Up in an Age of Unavoidable News

I hadn't expected to be writing about a military execution in a parenting newsletter. Then I started thinking about what kids might see or hear. The Defense Department says it will livestream the December 3 firing squad execution of the Fort Hood shooter, the first U.S. military execution in more than 60 years. There's plenty of debate about that decision, and I'll leave that to you.

As a parent, though, what I keep coming back to is what happens once the footage is online. The original livestream is only the beginning. There will be clips, reposts, reaction videos, and group chats passing it around. Some kids may go looking for it out of curiosity, and others may come across it without meaning to. We have almost two months before that happens, which gives parents something we rarely get with hard news:

Start with you.

Kids watch how we handle the news and copy it. If we stay calm, they're more likely to as well. And if something has really shaken you, it's OK to say so and talk about everyone's feelings, yours included.

Get your facts straight first.

Kids will have questions. Take a few minutes with credible news sources before the conversation, so you can decide what to share and how. Most kids don't need the details. What they're really looking for is reassurance.

What to say, by age:

Littles (7 and under):

Keep the news out of sight, including pop-up alerts on your own phone. Young kids can blur what's real with what they fear. Remind them they're safe and loved, and take their worries seriously.

Bigs & Tweens (8–12):

Ask what they've heard, since it likely came from friends or online. Be ready to explain bigger ideas like conflict and bias. More sensitive kids shouldn't marinate in coverage, because seeing the same stories over and over makes danger feel closer than it is.

The Problems with ChatGPT Teen Mode

In August, I shared our researchers' questions about the new ChatGPT for Teens. Now they've tested it, and the verdict is: Don't lean on it. A tester posing as a 13-year-old talked about self-harm, including a plan to end their life, for up to an hour, and no alert reached the parent. Testers who kept saying they were 13 were never switched into teen mode, and the homework guardrails could be skipped by deleting one tag. That's why our Youth AI Safety Institute rates ChatGPT an "Unacceptable Risk" for kids. A stark reminder that a "for Teens" label makes it easy to stop paying attention.

Tonight's to-do:

Ask your teen what they use ChatGPT for, and tell them plainly that it's not a friend or a crisis counselor. No setting replaces checking in with each other.

Spooky Season, Little-Kid Edition

October is peak season for little kids who

love

Halloween … right up until something actually gets scary. The fun comes when they get a tiny thrill while feeling completely safe on the couch next to you, and getting that balance right is the whole trick. One thing to keep in mind: Kids under 7 can't easily separate fantasy from reality, even when you tell them it's pretend. So what's a harmless giggle for a 10-year-old can mean a 3 a.m. visit from your kindergartner. Animated picks help, and so do stories about friendly mysteries rather than monsters.
