DOJ Urges Companies to Self Disclose Fraud and Avoid Prosecution

(Bloomberg) -- The head of the US Justice Department's Criminal Division is urging companies to self report possible fraud, favoring settlements with companies that proactively disclose potential violations over what could become costly and time-consuming prosecutions.

The approach has prompted criticism from some public-interest groups that President Donald Trump's second administration is being soft on crime, with one group publishing a report Wednesday listing the "cancellation" of more than 200 enforcement actions inherited from the Biden administration. Those actions include ending federal investigations, lawsuits, and settlements.

Assistant Attorney General Tysen Duva said in an interview with Bloomberg News that the approach is already paying off for the Justice Department. In the coming months, he said he will be announcing corporate enforcement policy resolutions, non-prosecution agreements and deferred-prosecution agreements with companies, and charges against individuals that were a result of self disclosures.

"It is better to try to obtain cooperation from an entity and provide incentives for them to come in and tell you what happened," Duva said. "Because if they don't, it's up to a whistleblower or a confidential informant. And then you get in this lengthy, protracted litigation."

Duva said if the Justice Department is able to convince companies to come in early and self disclose, his agency can truncate — and in some cases eliminate — a costly legal process. He said his emphasis on self-disclosure followed a precedent set before he was tapped to run the Criminal Division last year.

Duva was elevated from his previous post as a Jacksonville, Florida-based assistant US attorney in July of 2025, and confirmed by the Senate the following December.

"We really want to try to allay some fears and communicate very clearly that this means if you come in, and you self disclose, that you can obtain the declination, but you have to fully cooperate," Duva said. "It is laying out what you learned, how you learned it, and who did it. And as part of that, you would take that information and pursue charges against individuals."

The Justice Department pointed to a resolution reached in March with medical device company Balt SAS as an example a self-disclosure success. The department said the company disclosed and took steps to remediate the behavior. In return, DOJ declined to prosecute Balt as a company, instead bringing charges against two individuals. The company also agreed to pay $1.2 million in disgorgement.
