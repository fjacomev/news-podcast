This Digital Citizenship Week, Critical Thinking Is Key

Christine Elgersma

Senior Editor, Learning Content Strategy

Hi Educator,

As I was scrolling an article, a medical ad caught my eye. It was clearly a targeted ad, but for something I thought might actually be useful. Generally speaking, I don't click on ads, almost ever. But this one piqued my interest and looked legit. Once I got into the explainer, though, the text felt generic—

AI, maybe

? There was a doctor's name and picture (also generically suspect), so I looked her up. Only, there isn't a doctor by that name, anywhere. A reverse image search of the picture only led back to the ad. The entire endorsement for this product was made-up, including an imaginary doctor.

The whole process took about three minutes: see the ad, click through, skim, search for doc name, and reverse image-search the picture. But in the course of casual scrolling and interest in something, that's often three minutes we're not willing to spend. Thankfully, I already have the knowledge and critical thinking skills it takes to pause, think, and debunk, preventing

wasted money and even potential harm

Don't we want our students to have that knowledge and those skills?

I think so. The problem is, we're so busy with mandates and standards that there isn't much time for media literacy.

But this is bigger than media literacy.

This knowledge and those skills are an essential human capacity that actually

is

a part of mandates and standards: critical thinking.

It's easy to lose sight of our ultimate goals for students when we're caught up in standardized test prep, observations, and lockstep lesson plans. But learning how to think is actually the reason for so many of our curriculum requirements.

Students ask these questions all the time: "When am I ever going to use this in real life? How is this going to help me later?" It's not always easy to answer, except to say that

it helps them learn how to think

In this context, media literacy is an

easy

sell. It applies to their lives

right now

That's why defending kids' critical thinking in a world of AI is the focus of

Digital Citizenship Week

, which is next week!

This year, we're suggesting three activities for each grade band. The concepts in each (mostly offline) activity build upon one another, and each one should take about 20 to 40 minutes.

Hopefully, this very special week (now over a decade old!) can be the opportunity to dig into this essential topic. And we want to hear all about Digital Citizenship Week in your classroom!
