The Upside of Surging Bond Yields

Key Takeaways

The yield surge looks less like an inflation scare and more like a response to stronger growth, heavier borrowing and shifting Treasury demand.

Stocks have held up as earnings forecasts improve, though market leadership remains concentrated in AI-related technology companies.

The bond selloff may have gone too far—and if rates decline, today’s higher yields may offer both meaningful income and potential price gains.

For investors, a quality-focused approach may favor municipal bonds and large-company stocks with durable, achievable earnings growth.

Bond yields are flashing a message that investors can’t ignore. The 10-year Treasury yield recently hit a 24-year high of 5.3%, amid heavy volatility in fixed-income markets, raising a critical question: Is this a warning sign or an opportunity?

When yields rise, investors often see it as a harbinger of resurgent inflation. However, Morgan Stanley’s Global Investment Committee believes the yield surge is not mainly an inflation scare. Instead, it reflects stronger growth, heavier borrowing and shifting demand for bonds—forces that may have pushed up yields further than fundamentals justify.

That matters for portfolios. Higher yields can now offer compelling income, and if they decline as Morgan Stanley expects, high-quality bonds could also deliver price gains. Select stocks may benefit too, provided earnings keep improving.

Here’s how investors may want to think about the move.

Why Have Yields Been Surging?

Stable inflation expectations suggest price pressures are not the main reason yields have risen. Instead, investors appear to be responding to a mix of stronger U.S. growth, increased borrowing needs and a changing buyer base for Treasuries.

Growth has been more resilient than many expected, supported by investment in artificial intelligence, manufacturing and supply-chain resilience, as well as steady consumer spending. Stronger growth expectations can push rates higher even when inflation expectations remain contained.

At the same time, more debt issuance from governments and companies, including technology firms funding AI infrastructure, means bonds may need to offer higher yields to attract buyers.

A less supportive Fed may also be playing a role, as Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has signaled a move away from large-scale bond purchases and extensive forward guidance—tools that helped suppress long-term rates.

Demand is also shifting toward more price-sensitive private investors, who typically require higher yields when supply rises or uncertainty increases.

How Have Rising Rates Affected U.S. Stocks?

As surging yields roil bond markets, they can also weigh on stocks by raising companies’ financing costs, making bonds more competitive with equities and reducing how much investors are willing to pay for future profit growth.

That pressure has not been felt evenly: Megacap technology stocks, along with software and semiconductor companies tied to AI, have helped support the broader market, while financials, value stocks and small-cap stocks have lagged.

Even so, headline indexes have largely taken the rate surge in stride, helped by continued optimism about corporate profits. Analysts now expect S&P 500 companies to earn between $415 and $420 per share in 2027, a substantial 12% increase in their forecast since the start of the year. With earnings forecasts rising even as higher rates pressure valuations, the S&P 500’s forward price-to-earnings ratio has fallen from about 22.5 at the start of the year to roughly 19—leaving parts of the stock market looking more attractively priced.

What Could Higher Yields Mean for Your Portfolio?

Morgan Stanley’s Global Investment Committee sees opportunities in both bonds and stocks, with an emphasis on quality, income and selectivity.

Bonds: We believe the bond selloff has gone too far. Markets are pricing in about three Federal Reserve rate hikes through next December, while Morgan Stanley analysts expect only two. We see the 10-year Treasury yield gradually declining toward 4.7% over the next 12 to 15 months. For investors, today’s yields above 5% may offer meaningful income and help cushion portfolios against volatility. If rates decline as expected, bond prices could rise as well. Municipal bonds, in particular, may offer attractive income and potential tax advantages for eligible investors.

Stocks: We are also constructive on stocks. The price investors are paying for expected corporate earnings has declined, leaving parts of the equity market more reasonably valued, even as major indexes have reached record highs. Still, selectivity matters. We favor high-quality, large-cap growth companies with earnings forecasts they appear well positioned to achieve. Our S&P 500 targets are 8,000 by year-end and 8,300 by next June.
