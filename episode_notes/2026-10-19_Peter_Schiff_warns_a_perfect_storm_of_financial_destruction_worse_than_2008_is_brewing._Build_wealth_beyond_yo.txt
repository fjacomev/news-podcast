The low mortgage rate you locked in years ago might be one of your best financial decisions. Peter Schiff argued it could also be part of a "perfect storm of financial destruction that is about to hit."

"Mortgage lenders will lose far more during the developing mortgage crisis than they did during all of the GFC," Schiff warned in an Oct. 15 post on X (1), referring to the global financial crisis.

He claimed, "Even without a single default, on paper mortgage lenders have already lost over four times more than they lost during the GFC."

He blamed the Federal Reserve's prolonged low interest rates and government-backed mortgages that let borrowers lock in cheap financing for decades.

"That sowed the seeds," he wrote.

An alarming forecast, especially if your home represents much of your wealth or you're counting on your investments to carry you through retirement.

But before you start picturing another foreclosure crisis, there's an important distinction: A mortgage losing value to an investor doesn't automatically mean your house has lost value, your payment is going up, or your lender is about to fail.

And keep in mind, if your loan or its servicing changes hands, the new company can't just rewrite your agreement (2).

Why Schiff sees trouble in low-rate mortgages

"There is currently about $8.3 trillion in outstanding fixed-rate mortgages with an average rate of 4.6% and 25 years left to maturity," Schiff wrote. "Given that current rates are 7.5%, the mark-to-market losses on those mortgages are already about $2 trillion. When rates rise to 8.5%, the loss will rise to $3 trillion!"

For comparison, Schiff put realized losses on defaulted mortgages during 2007–2010 at about $450 billion.

Keep in mind these are Schiff's estimates, and his 8.5% scenario isn't today's reality. The latest Freddie Mac survey (3), released Oct. 15, put the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.40%, up from 6.27% a year earlier.

Here's the basic pricing problem: If a new mortgage pays a substantially higher rate, an investor generally won't pay full price for an otherwise comparable older loan paying much less. The older loan then needs a discount to compete.

That decline in market value is the paper loss Schiff is describing. The homeowner could still be making every payment on time.

Schiff puts two different types of losses side by side — money lost when borrowers default, and declines in what performing loans could fetch in a sale.

A mortgage can lose resale value even while homeowners keep making payments. That can hurt whoever owns the loan, but it isn't the same as a borrower defaulting.

Who owns the loan matters. Mortgages are often sold and packaged into securities (4) whose investors receive payments from pools of borrowers. So, the company that originated your mortgage isn't necessarily the one carrying its investment risk going forward.

You don't have to accept Schiff's prediction to examine your own exposure. If your home accounts for much of your wealth, how much have you built outside it?
