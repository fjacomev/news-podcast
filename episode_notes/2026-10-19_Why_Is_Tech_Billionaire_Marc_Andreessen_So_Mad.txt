# Why Is Tech Billionaire Marc Andreessen So Mad?

October 15, 2026

The prominent investor has said the Biden administration pushed him to the political right, but sources say his grievances don't always match reality.

By Austin Carr

Marc Andreessen sounded angry. It was May 22, 2024, and he was venting to a table full of President Joe Biden's officials in the West Wing office of Jeff Zients, then the White House chief of staff. The famed venture capitalist was in town with his longtime investing partner, Ben Horowitz, to discuss US tech policy, and the private lunch soon turned heated. Andreessen expressed his frustration about a slew of Biden administration initiatives and regulations, especially ones he believed were crippling the artificial intelligence and cryptocurrency industries. Near the end of the lunch, he also launched into a tirade against a Democratic proposal to hike taxes on the wealthiest 0.01% of American households. "The billionaire tax seemed to offend him more than anything we'd done on AI or even crypto," says Bruce Reed, one of the handful of White House advisers who had joined the meeting.

Forgotten, at least, until Donald Trump won the election months later and Andreessen revealed in the aftermath the crazy reason why he had unexpectedly plowed millions of dollars into the Make America Great Again movement during the campaign. Andreessen, who voted for Hillary Clinton in 2016 and said back then that Trump's immigration regime "makes me sick to my stomach," explained on a podcast that he was compelled by "absolutely horrifying" discussions he once had with Biden officials. In his retelling, they divulged a top-secret plan for the federal government to take control of the AI industry and outlaw all startups and open-source software. "You guys think you can just start companies and write code and release code on the internet—those days are over," he remembered them warning in the meeting.

Andreessen was so aghast that he felt it was in the best interest of his firm, Andreessen Horowitz, which is known as a16z, to publicly endorse Trump. "They're going to kill us," Andreessen recalled thinking on Ross Douthat's podcast in January 2025. "We walked out and stood in the parking lot of the West Wing and took one look at each other, and we're like, 'Yeah, we're for Trump.'" (Horowitz later hedged his support when Kamala Harris replaced Biden in the race, and he made a significant personal donation.)

The incident became Silicon Valley lore, but at first the Biden-Harris camp couldn't even figure out what meeting Andreessen was referring to, according to four people familiar with the situation. When they finally realized he was talking about the lunch in Zients' office, those who attended were baffled: Andreessen is prone to hyperbole, but this neared complete fiction. "It is simply false that the Biden administration told Andreessen and Horowitz that the government would ban all AI startups," says Ben Buchanan, then a special adviser to the president on AI, who attended the May 22 meeting. It also made little sense why Andreessen hadn't asked about this astonishingly draconian plan at a meeting on May 24 with national security advisers including Anne Neuberger. (Neuberger, whom a16z has since hired, declined to comment.) As another top Biden official familiar with their visits told colleagues of Andreessen's version of events: "Total bulls---."

A representative for Andreessen, Horowitz and a16z's government affairs chief, Collin McCune, who also attended the contentious meeting in DC, has said the firm stands by their recollections. (Horowitz and McCune didn't respond to requests for comment for this article.) Whatever actually occurred at that lunch matters less than what's happened since: a16z, known for Andreessen once proclaiming that "software is eating the world," is now eating the political world. The dominant VC firm, which manages more than $100 billion in assets, has gone from Valley kingmaker to DC power broker. Through political action committees and funding tied to its co-founders and partners, the Andreessen Horowitz team has poured at least $116 million into federal politics since Trump's reelection. They have contributed more to campaign efforts for the coming midterms than George Soros, Miriam Adelson or Charles Koch.

A16z generally supports candidates whose views align with its priorities in AI and crypto. But since choosing Trump, Andreessen has contributed more in party-aligned donations than he did in the preceding three decades combined, with roughly 94% going to Republicans, including $6 million for the MAGA Inc. super PAC. This shift remains a subject of debate. In interviews, current and former a16z partners, firm-backed startup founders, fellow board members and VCs, political associates and old friends describe Andreessen as an undeniably brilliant industrialist, but also someone who is perpetually driven by grievances that don't quite match reality. Most of these people asked not to be named, to avoid getting on the wrong side of one of the most influential people in Silicon Valley.

One illustrious a16z-funded founder, who hails Andreessen as likely the "highest IQ" investor on the planet, says it never made sense why he seemed to hold such resentment toward the very institutions that helped him achieve the American dream. "It's like, dude, you've made billions of dollars," this founder says. "How can you think the government is f---ed up and everything is wrong with America when it's obviously worked better for you than it has for all but maybe a dozen people in human history?"

These days, Andreessen, 55, splits his time between his California estates. In Malibu, he has a $34 million beach house that's held in an LLC called Paradise Shack, but he prefers to do his extensive podcasting at a separate beach house down the road valued at $44.5 million. That's when he's not at his $177 million compound, also in Malibu, which has oceanfront guesthouses, caretaker quarters, a cinema and spa, or his Bay Area properties in Atherton. Aside from special events, such as Jeff Bezos's 2025 wedding, Andreessen, his wife and their two children spend most of their time as a family at home, according to two people familiar with their living situation. When the family does venture out, they often bring their security guards.

Andreessen came from humbler beginnings. He grew up on the piney edges of New Lisbon, Wisconsin, a small town about two hours from Milwaukee. His childhood home was a split-level about the footprint of one of his swimming pools today. It still sits on a shallow lake inlet and hasn't changed much since the Reagan era. For years after escaping, Andreessen professed to hate talking about his youth there, and rarely did.

For a renowned native son who's lately been ascribing his moral reawakening to his proud Wisconsin heritage, it's remarkable how few vestiges of him remain there. There's no bronze plaque commemorating his role in the tech boom in New Lisbon's tired downtown. Residents say he pretty much disappeared long before he was pictured on a throne barefoot on the cover of Time magazine in 1996. Neighbors on Cherokee Lane say they had no clue the creator of Netscape, which ushered in the internet age, originally learned to program next door.

The only visible sign of Andreessen's Midwestern past is hidden at the Beer Hunter, about 20 minutes away, in the village of Hustler. In the bar's bathroom, above the original, is a nostalgic snapshot of a teenage Andreessen plucking his electric guitar with a wide grin and "Buddy frickin' Holly hairdo," as owner Kelly Deasy puts it. Deasy was good friends with Marc growing up in the 1980s. He fondly remembers jamming with him after school, summers occasionally swimming at the lake behind his house and playing Dungeons & Dragons together. Andreessen loved concocting detailed definitions for new D&D inventory items, including rings of power that allowed your character to shape-shift or command creatures with fewer than 13 points of intelligence. "Marc was always the dungeon master. He was great at telling stories," Deasy says.

It's still a mystery to many classmates why Andreessen, until very recently, expressed such acrimony toward his upbringing. It wasn't unusual for kids there to dream of a less boring life, but Andreessen has said in interviews that Midwestern culture is "all wrong," rife with "subsistence farmers" and "self-denying people who go through life never expecting to be happy." He's described having to chop wood one winter to heat the family home and suffering through public schools with meager resources and no advanced classes. His relationship with his parents, Patricia and Lowell, was apparently so fraught that he refuses to speak of them. "His childhood was so intensely bad he just won't go there," Horowitz once told the New Yorker.

Dozens of longtime residents and family friends, however, describe New Lisbon as a normal slice of Middle America. Richard Hoile says Andreessen was the smartest kid in their class and was selected in fourth grade for a program to give gifted students early access to the high school's new computer. He taught himself to code from a library book and persuaded his parents to buy him a computer from Radio Shack. Lowell, a sales manager for an agriculture company, even had a bulky dial-up device for booking seed orders remotely that Marc loved showing to his old buddies. "The first modem I ever saw," says Andy Kurtz of the device. "He pulled out this briefcase and said, 'This allows me to connect to computers anywhere in the world!' "

People close to the Andreessens say they were on the middle-class end of the blue-collar town and that Lowell and Pat were incredibly nice folks who supported Marc's tech passions. Family friend Ken Southworth also notes that the town is so tiny that if there ever was trouble at home or "this or that SOB" mistreated Marc, word would've gotten out that could explain his puzzling animosity toward his parents and New Lisbon. "But there's nothing-flat nothing," Southworth insists.

New Lisbon's high school had two computer labs with Apple IIs and IBMs, as well as classes devoted to teaching spreadsheets, word processing and other software applications. They were rudimentary, but Andreessen, who was more advanced than the teachers, was pretty much given free rein. "Marc acts like he was treated so poorly. That's bulls---!" says Kim Dvorak, who says she always sat behind Andreessen in school. Archived yearbooks and newspapers capture an ordinary community supporting the wunderkind. He was a top 10 student, a member of the math club and Model UN and quiz bowl team, class treasurer and a Youth Governor for a county judge. He won a $2,000 scholarship sponsored by Eastman Kodak Co. his senior year, and he was named a young scholar by the same NSF that he now says deserves the bureaucratic death penalty.

He attended the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, which then ranked second only to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in engineering and was one of five institutions in the US chosen to host an NSF-funded supercomputer program. Andreessen worked at the school's National Center for Supercomputing Applications in the early 1990s, where he helped create the groundbreaking Mosaic browser, which made the World Wide Web accessible to the masses.

Soon, Andreessen was off to California for an $80,000 tech job. Then, with Silicon Graphics Inc. founder Jim Clark, he co-founded Mosaic Communications Corp., as if they were authorized to build the commercial edition of the university's Mosaic program. He and Clark ransacked NCSA talent to release the Mosaic Netscape browser. (After a legal dispute with Andreessen's alma mater, the startup dropped the Mosaic branding.)

Netscape, which retailed for $39 a copy, rapidly eclipsed the original Mosaic and went public a mere 10 months after its beta launch. The 24-year-old Andreessen was suddenly worth $50 million. His parents stopped hearing from him around that time. They said in newspaper interviews they were proud of their son and missed him, but they wouldn't follow him to California. His younger brother, Jeff, told Businessweek in 1998 that their childhood was a happy one and he didn't understand why Marc hadn't spoken to him in four years. When reached recently by phone, Lowell, who still lives in the area, says he cannot continue the conversation and asks never to be called again.

Deasy, the Beer Hunter owner, recalls sending Marc a congratulatory email after his early success, joking that “it wasn’t enough for him to take over New Lisbon. He also had to take over the world.” Andreessen never responded.

In many ways, Andreessen, an early mentor to Mark Zuckerberg, was the original hoodie-wearing tech bro. In the Netscape days, he wore baggy shorts, played Doom at the office, emailed through the night, and lived off SpaghettiOs and other junk food. (Netscape's subsequently hired chief executive officer said the 6-foot-4 Andreessen ate burgers "like a horse, both in quantity and mannerisms.") The company struggled to compete with Microsoft Corp.'s Internet Explorer, the web browser that came standard on Windows and later proved central to the US Department of Justice's landmark antitrust case against the tech giant. AOL acquired Netscape for $4.2 billion in stock a year before the dot-com bubble burst.

Andreessen acted as a caricature of himself online, averaging more than 100 tweets a day, frequently about inane memes or provocative topics. But he exuded more gravitas in person. The firm's Sand Hill Road office has a modernist aesthetic, and Andreessen enjoyed clinking glasses of Highland Park 18 scotch on Fridays with his partners and studied out-of-print tomes on niche subjects in business history. He has said he thinks of himself as a “Midwestern engineer tinkerer” counterbalancing the tech industry’s “acid-dropping” culture. This self-conception came from a 1983 Esquire magazine profile he deeply loved of Robert Noyce, the legendary co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and then Intel Corp. In the article, the author, Tom Wolfe, describes how Noyce ventured to California with his Iowa values “sewn into the lining of his coat” and became known as the “Mayor of Silicon Valley.”

Andreessen treated the founders he backed like A-list Hollywood clients, and they insist he can be incredibly caring behind the scenes. Patrick Collison, CEO of fintech juggernaut Stripe Inc., remembers once falling seriously ill prior to a group dinner at Andreessen's home and hurrying to Stanford Hospital. Collison texted his apologies for having to miss the dinner, and then was shocked when Andreessen showed up at his recovery room. “Marc didn’t just come by to give best wishes,” Collison says. “He spent three hours sitting with me in that little room.”

Andreessen's insights were invaluable on company boards. One colleague was surprised how "linear" Andreessen's mind was in a corporate governance role, unlike the wackier role he played online. A different board director says Andreessen is "a joy" to serve with: a great listener and pattern-matcher who isn't afraid to vigorously challenge others, usually with humor. "For those who don't know him, he's clearly intimating," this director says. "For those of us who know him, we're always waiting in anticipation for him to inject his viewpoint."

A big appeal of a16z was that it offered expert resources for functions like marketing and sales, but political lobbying was not originally on the menu. Andreessen played the game himself and made no bones about switching allegiances when expedient. He donated $250,000 in support of Vice President Al Gore in 2000, and then threatened to drop support for John Kerry four years later after the senator sought to kill tax incentives for companies outsourcing jobs to China. After a gushing 1,500-word blog endorsement of Barack Obama in 2008, he threw his weight behind Mitt Romney in the next presidential election.

By the 2016 presidential race, Andreessen was already questioning his political assumptions. He later told Joe Rogan and other podcasters that Trump's victory set off a phase of introspection during which he concluded that he had become too assimilated to California and needed to get back to his Wisconsin values. But people close to him say his conversion wasn't so immediate. Andreessen had actually expressed concern years earlier that Valley-driven social upheaval was likely inevitable, whether due to data privacy issues or growing monopoly power in the tech industry.

Yet Andreessen and his team were far from right-wing culture warriors during Trump's first term. If anything, they remained relatively progressive. While Andreessen said at a tech conference in 2017 that Breitbart's news coverage had a better read on Middle America than coastal elites did, he also agreed onstage with investor Reid Hoffman that it was vital to build bridges between the country's factions. The next year, a16z introduced a Cultural Leadership Fund to increase Black representation in the tech industry, with Diddy and Will Smith as limited partners. Horowitz, a friend and champion of then-Senator Kamala Harris, continued advocating for DEI causes through her 2020 presidential primary campaign.

A friend and former VC associate says any personal transformation for Andreessen around this period seemed more driven by business than politics, especially as the Securities and Exchange Commission ramped up pressure on the crypto industry, which a16z had invested in significantly. Scotch Fridays dried up as the firm ballooned from a few dozen employees to several hundred. This friend sensed Andreessen becoming more guarded as a16z's stature grew and says the “real Marc” was only accessible to a dwindling circle of friends. “His public persona is a lot less human than his personal one,” the friend says. “But I think you get to see the ‘real Marc’ when he's with his family. You'd be surprised. He's just a regular guy.”

arc and his wife, Laura Arrillaga-Andreessen, stayed put at their Atherton compound during the Covid-19 pandemic. Their household staff normally numbers in the dozens, including personal assistants, chefs, housekeepers and nannies, according to three people familiar with the setup. But like everyone else, they found the ordeal excruciating. Laura was already fastidious about germs, and Andreessen would get pains in his neck from marathon Zoom calls at the dining table.

He coped with walks around Stanford University's deserted campus, where Laura's surname adorns almost a dozen buildings and landmarks. (Her father, billionaire Silicon Valley real estate developer John Arrillaga, was one of Stanford's largest benefactors and a hero of Marc's.) It was a radicalizing time. He loathed what he saw as the government's catastrophic unpreparedness and ineffective policies, not to mention that damn Stanford security guard who kept yelling at him from a distance to put his mask back on as he marched by outside.

Andreessen, who complained about tech-platform censorship, began opening up in private group chats with like-minded colleagues in tech and politics. They fumed about what they saw as anti-police hysteria after George Floyd's death and argued it was ludicrous that Democratic officials and public-health experts supported 2020's Black Lives Matter protests in spite of their opposition to large public gatherings. One Republican participant says Andreessen would routinely get “activated” by some hot-button issue, engage in hours of text-debating through “long, long, long group chat essays” and then often delete everything when the conversation died.

Not long after he and Laura bought their Malibu estate in the exclusive beach enclave of Paradise Cove—then the most expensive home purchase in California history—people close to him noticed a real change. Amid all the podcast talk of reembracing his Wisconsin values, he seemed to become more withdrawn from the outside world, one estranged tech colleague says. He and Laura, who was undergoing her own anti-DEI conversion, homeschooled their young son and even hired a mixed martial arts coach to teach him to fight in an octagon cage erected in one of their backyards. (Andreessen has since said publicly that such training is necessary in an increasingly violent America.)

Andreessen's grievances worsened throughout Biden's term in office. He eventually started fulminating publicly about suppressed masculinity, waning patriotism and the "woke mind virus." To some, the sentiments came across as opportunistic posturing as Biden's approval ratings collapsed and SEC Chair Gary Gensler and Federal Trade Commission Chair Lina Khan upset a16z partners with their aggressive crypto enforcement actions and merger-and-acquisition reviews.

Richard Hanania, a right-wing political scientist who Andreessen asked in 2022 to put together a Signal chat of smart conservatives, noticed Andreessen's views growing more extreme that year. He recalls the small group, which Tucker Carlson later joined, becoming an echo chamber of MAGA tribalism. It felt to him as if Andreessen's principles were mostly driven by a reactive dislike of liberals, the media and really anyone challenging his tech efforts. "By 2023, Marc just had it with anyone who he didn't see as sufficiently loyal to the Trump movement," says Hanania, who soon left the Signal chat. (Andreessen has since blocked him on X.)

Max Levchin, who co-founded PayPal Holdings Inc. and is now the CEO of a16z-backed Affirm Inc., has looked up to Andreessen ever since they crossed paths at the University of Illinois. Levchin has watched his tech friends shift to the right in recent years. He says that Andreessen distinguishes himself with his "moral backbone" on polarizing political issues. At the same time, Andreessen is "completely unafraid to take a 90- or 180-degree turn and say, 'This is my new conviction,'" Levchin says. "At the most profound level possible, he's willing to learn and be like, 'Oh, I was wrong. Now I believe this is the truth, and everyone else can go f--- themselves."'

In May 2023, a16z said it committed $500 million to its new focus on what it calls American Dynamism, which involves investments in homegrown companies in defense tech and other industries of strategic importance, such as Anduril Industries Inc. and SpaceX. Also in this portfolio is Flock Safety, which makes surveillance technologyfor police departments and commercial organizations. CEO Garrett Langley says no VCs wanted to get near Flock because of its third-rail mission, glossing over the fact that Y Combinator, Peter Thiel's Founders Fund and five other institutional investors had backed the startup before a16z. "There was a realization that someone has to stand up and say the only way America is going to be a better place to live is via technology," Langley says, praising Andreessen and Horowitz. "They're willing to put their money where their mouth is."

Andreessen codified his ethos in his viral "Techno-Optimist Manifesto," a lengthy October 2023 screed that heralded "technological supermen" as the engines of progress and cast central government planners as cloggers of this free-market machine. Andreessen, who has long lionized Gilded Age titans such as Andrew Carnegie and John Pierpont Morgan, wrote of the greatness of the "industrialists who came before us" and a yearning to make them proud. The manifesto cited Atlas Shrugged protagonist John Galt as one of its "patron saints" and railed against "energies" of progress, especially "know-it-all" experts "indulging in abstract theories, luxury beliefs, social engineering, disconnected from the real world, delusional, unelected, and unaccountable—playing God with everyone else's lives, with total insulation from the consequences."

Maybe not coincidentally, Andreessen published his manifesto a week before participating in a bipartisan Senate forum discussing how the US should govern AI. Also in attendance were some of the kinds of people Andreessen had labeled as enemies in his manifesto, setting up a potential confrontation. Andreessen recalled recently on X that Max Tegmark, an AI safety researcher at MIT, “pound[ed] the table” in front of the senators, “demanding open source AI software be made illegal.”

Tegmark says that's totally false. Five other attendees agree, saying the closed-door meeting was cordial and uneventful; Andreessen, they remember, didn't say much at all. It seemed to reflect a decades-long pattern of tactical fabulism. Aside from being a replay of the disputed descriptions of the 2024 White House lunch, the episode echoed an event that helped spark the government's monopoly case against Microsoft some 30 years ago. Andreessen, then at Netscape, sent his notes from a routine business meeting with Microsoft executives to the DOJ, claiming that Microsoft had explicitly threatened to annihilate his company if he didn't cede control of the browser market. Microsoft execs who attended the meeting said they learned of these claims only years later and were bewildered, swearing that Andreessen's recollections were nonsense.

Perhaps because of its framing of technologists as surrounded by such threats, the Techno-Optimist Manifesto was a hit among people inspired by Andreessen's refrain that it's finally “time to build.” In private, though, some big-name technologists and VCs say they found the 5,000-word essay somewhat ridiculous. (Even Thiel called the piece “pure Silicon Valley gobbledygook propaganda,” according to a leaked recording from an off-the-record talk in San Francisco.) It clearly repackaged tenets of effective accelerationism, or “e/acc,” a trendy pro-tech philosophy that argues in favor of unfettered innovation and against AI doomerism.

Four of the many a16z-backed startup founders interviewed for this story describe Andreessen's manifesto as weirdly angry and oversimplistic. "Marc really does believe that all you have to do is build stuff and lives will improve, at the expense of nuance about how the real world operates and these things affect real people," says one of these founders, noting that government intervention has hardly impeded tech leaders from accumulating trillions of dollars of wealth. "It's not going to end well for this country if we keep going down this path."

Andreessen and Horowitz first met Trump in July 2024 for dinner at his New Jersey golf club. They hit it off, people familiar with their interactions say, and Vice President JD Vance further persuaded Andreessen to contribute to the Trump 2.0 movement. His Wisconsin backstory and bet on American Dynamism played well at donor summits. In August 2024, at House Speaker Mike Johnson's private GOP retreat in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, one attendee says Andreessen impressed the crowd with a 40-minute fireside chat about a16z's investments in military tech and AI breakthroughs. "He kept repeating that he only invests in companies that he firmly believes will help the country," the attendee recalls.

After Trump's election win that November, Andreessen spent a lot of time at Mar-a-Lago helping with the transition. (He jokingly referred to his position there as "unpaid intern.") This included vetting DOGE hires and candidates for jobs at other government agencies. A source close to Andreessen says he was one of the loudest voices urging Trump to pick conservative investor David Sacks, the PayPal-veteran-turned-All-In podcaster, as his czar on AI and crypto, key priorities for a16z. Trump also appointed Scott Kupor, a16z's first employee and longtime managing partner, to the influential post of director of the Office of Personnel Management.

What's stood out to observers is how Andreessen has managed to stay in the president's good graces for so long, given Trump's tendency to expel those he suspects are putting their interests before his. Where Musk has proved unpredictable on this front, Andreessen has gained a reputation as a rather shrewd diplomat willing to prove his loyalties. He was spotted cageside with Zuckerberg at the Ultimate Fighting Championship's White House spectacular in June. During this year's government deliberations over AI safety, he surprised some by encouraging Wiles to ease tensions with leaders from Anthropic PBC (the main rival of a16z-backed OpenAI), who Trump had branded "Leftwing nut jobs" on Truth Social. A person who's closely observed Andreessen's political interactions recalls how "utterly effusive" he was in talking with Trump officials, even thanking them at times for their service to the country.

This treatment has led to tangible victories in crypto for a16z, whose funds in the space total almost $10 billion. Pressure from the SEC has largely disappeared under Trump, and Congress handed Andreessen a big win in July 2025 when it passed a friendly national framework for stablecoins.

Equally important for a16z has been stamping out state laws on AI while advocating for lighter-touch federal oversight, a position Sacks has championed. For a state-level AI safety bill under consideration in New York, a16z lobbied to exempt “small” startups, which it defined as those with as much as $50 billion in annual AI revenue, a threshold that would have then excused virtually every company on the planet. When the law passed without the exemption, an a16z-allied super PAC poured millions of dollars into defeating Alex Bores, one of the bill's authors, in his recent run for Congress. After Senate Democrats helped block a new pro-crypto bill, another affiliated PAC said it would invest $30 million to campaign against Ohio Senate candidate Sherrod Brown, a critic of the crypto industry.

A former Trump official says it's become clear that Andreessen's favorite AI policy is no policy, particularly with the debates this fall about whether the federal government should impose a pause in the development of frontier AI models or introduce a system for evaluating safety threats. Beyond AI and crypto, Andreessen mostly seemed keen to play “demolition derby” with any institutions he believed had somehow wronged him, says the ex-official, who often couldn't understand the origin of his grievances. “Marc's two top principles are supporting builders in this Randian quest against bureaucracy, and revenge,” this person explains. “Ultimately Marc comes down more on the side of ‘F--- my enemies.’”

Other critics worry that Andreessen is indulging in luxury beliefs, as his manifesto warned that his enemies tend to do, playing God with everyone else's life while insulated from the consequences. The Andreessen family still spends much of its time in the bubble of their California compounds, according to people close to them.

Andreessen's old friends and neighbors in New Lisbon remain blown away by his accomplishments. They are hopeful that his renewed interest in his Wisconsin roots will bring some of his techno-optimist prosperity back to their hometown, especially as he's talked more lately about wanting to help "farm boys" like himself achieve success in America. Yet in all the years since he left, town leaders, who have continuously tried contacting Andreessen to seek support for the rural community, say he's never responded and that they're not aware of him ever giving back to the area save an unpaid $25,000 reward he once offered for information on a teacher who went missing in 1987 and was later found dead. The local school and library where Andreessen first learned to program have been renovated, but his name is not on the list of donors. (Andreessen, who is on the advisory board for his wife's nonprofit Institute on Generosity, griped recently that the government has made it illegal for rich people to build public libraries the way Carnegie once did.) One resident says the town is "stuck in the 1970s."

And unlike his Midwestern tinkerer idol, Robert Noyce, Andreessen hasn't given anything back to his alma mater either. The original "Mayor of Silicon Valley" contributed significantly to the Iowa college that nurtured him, but Andreessen is still bitter about that decades-old legal spat with the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, which forced him to drop the Mosaic name and assure the university he hadn't copied any code for Netscape. In a statement, the current dean of UIUC's college of engineering says Andreessen remains an inspiration to students, adding: "We regret that we didn't do a better job of celebrating him and his entrepreneurship at that time."

If Andreessen's June 2024 comments about the situation are any indication, he's not ready to move forward. "To be totally honest, I'm still upset. Every subsequent administration, every subsequent new administration at the university has attempted to reach out and repair the bridge. I have not returned the calls," Andreessen told Horowitz on their podcast. "I greatly value my grudges. They're very important to me."
