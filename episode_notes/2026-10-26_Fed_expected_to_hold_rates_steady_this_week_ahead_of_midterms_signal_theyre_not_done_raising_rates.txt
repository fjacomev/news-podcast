Fed expected to hold rates steady this week despite hot inflation — but signal they're not done hiking

The Federal Reserve heads into its interest-rate decision this week facing hotter inflation, keeping the meeting live even as markets widely expect the central bank to hold interest rates steady just days before the midterm elections.

Alan Blinder, a former Fed vice chair and a Princeton University economics professor, is expecting a "hawkish pause."

Although Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh doesn't favor telegraphing the central bank's next move, Blinder said the moment calls for clarity.

"This is a perfect time for clear forward guidance that doesn't quite say, but strongly hints, we're pausing now, but we stand ready to raise again and even give a hint that the next meeting might be the next interest rate hike."

Inflation heated up in September, as the Consumer Price Index accelerated to 3.7% from a year earlier. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, ticked up to 2.6%, while wholesale prices jumped more than 6%, and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — the core Personal Consumption Expenditures index — is projected to top 3%.

In his last remarks before this week's policy meeting, many observers think Warsh underscored what Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and New York Fed president John Williams previously said: that despite hot inflation reports, they would like to take time to review the data as they consider the prospect of further rate hikes.

Warsh neither endorsed nor pushed back on his colleagues' recent signals that they'd like to wait for more data, but he noted that the world had "broadly conformed to our expectations" since the Fed's September meeting.

After raising rates for the first time in more than three years in September, minutes from that meeting read that most participants assessed that another increase would likely be appropriate by year-end.

Ken Rogoff, Harvard University professor and former chief economist of the International Monetary Fund, told Yahoo Finance he thinks the Fed will hold rates steady next week because of the proximity to the midterm elections less than a week later.

"The fundamentals call for raising the rate," Rogoff said. "If you didn't have an election, they should hike … But they are going to hold rates steady and talk about how they're probably going to increase it once or twice at least in the near future."

Blinder agrees that the midterms are part of the consideration. "When it's that close to an election there's something to be said for keeping your head down," he said, noting that while it is far from the only factor, election proximity serves as a "minus" in the mental basket for raising rates.
