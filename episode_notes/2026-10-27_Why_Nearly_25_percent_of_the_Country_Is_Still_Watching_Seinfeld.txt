Why Nearly 25% of the Country Is Still Watching ‘Seinfeld’

By David Rosenthal & Ben Gilbert

October 23, 2026

wsj.com

At the time, Welch was the CEO of General Electric and the most famous and respected corporate head in America. (Fortune magazine would soon name him the “Manager of the Century”.)

But for Welch, the purpose of this brunch wasn’t to hobnob with a celebrity. It was to close a business deal. GE owned NBC, and “Seinfeld” was its critical moneymaker, generating $200 million in profit annually, or around 20% of NBC’s total operating income. The network had already made Seinfeld—the show’s eponymous star, co-creator and writer—the highest-paid TV talent in history at that point, at $1 million per episode. But Seinfeld had recently been sending signals that the show’s current season might be his last, and of course there was no way the show could continue without him.

As the meal ended and talk turned to business, Welch wasn’t worried. He’d come armed with a time-honored, foolproof strategy: an offer Seinfeld couldn’t refuse. Welch took out his pen, scribbled a note and handed it to Seinfeld. $5 million…per episode. Which at 22 episodes would equate to $110 million for the season. (In 1998 dollars!) Surely Seinfeld wouldn’t be fool enough to turn down that kind of money.

But as we all know, Seinfeld, exhausted from the painstaking process of making the show, did turn down the offer. “Seinfeld” went off NBC’s airwaves in May 1998 after its nine-season run.

In the decades since, observers have framed the decision as one of artistic integrity: “going out on top” triumphing over cold business logic. In a famous 2017 interview with Seinfeld, Harvard Business Review even suggested that perhaps McKinsey consultants could have helped him find a way to continue the show.

It’s a nice characterization: a rare and laudable example of a creator choosing art above money. However, it’s wrong. It was the correct choice for both art and money.

And as we explore on the latest episode of ‘Acquired’, it was this exact decision that contributes to “Seinfeld’s” astonishing relevance today, three decades after it aired. Nearly a quarter of all Americans, or 72 million people, watched at least one “Seinfeld” episode in the first eight months of 2026, according to ratings-agency Nielsen.

But let’s rewind for a bit of context: what Welch knew at that brunch—but most of the “Seinfeld” viewing public didn’t understand—was that NBC didn’t own “Seinfeld.” It couldn’t. Because when the show began in 1989, the FCC’s “Financial Interest and Syndication,” or “fin-syn,” rules were still in place, and they prevented broadcast networks from owning the television shows that they aired in prime time.

As a result, shows were owned and produced by independent production companies, and networks like NBC paid a license fee to air episodes. Star creators were free to cut their own “back-end” deals with these production companies, which would grant direct ownership stakes in future syndication rights. (This practice was later greatly diminished when the FCC voted to end fin-syn in 1993.)

Seinfeld—along with his “Seinfeld” co-creator and partner Larry David—had negotiated one of the industry’s best back-end deals with the show’s production company, Castle Rock Entertainment, reportedly granting them each 15% of syndication profits.

Now, for most TV shows, syndication rights are basically worthless. The local TV stations and cable networks (and later, online streamers) who buy syndicated programming don’t have much interest in paying to license shows that only ran for a couple seasons and never caught on. But for hit shows with deep libraries, bidding wars could erupt that pushed revenue for the 5-year rights to a show, called a syndication cycle, into the hundreds of millions.

By the time Welch and Seinfeld sat down in late 1997, “Seinfeld” had already sold its first syndication cycle, which fetched an estimated then-record $500 million in revenue. Typically, revenue would decline in subsequent syndication cycles after the show ceased production and public interest waned.

But “Seinfeld” was shaping up to be an exception.

During its first syndication run, odd things began happening. The show’s audience grew. A local TV station would start showing “Seinfeld” reruns, and it would outperform all other channels in the same timeslot…even when pitted against live and first-run shows! Suddenly the surest path to growing market share for a local station was no longer hiring the most popular news anchors or broadcasting the local college’s sporting events, but securing “Seinfeld” rights.

It turned out a show where characters never learn from their mistakes made every episode independently watchable—a perfect recipe for syndication. There was something intrinsic about this show that kept America wanting more of it. As the show’s second cycle approached, it looked like it might draw more even demand than the first. Perhaps significantly more.

When Seinfeld turned down Welch’s offer, it was clear that the show’s syndication future remained bright. The question of “how bright?” was hypersensitive to one factor: longevity. If “Seinfeld” could sustain this level of syndication demand for decades, $110 million would look paltry in comparison to future syndication profits.

Seinfeld would recall later: “I really didn’t think about the money at all. I thought about the audience. How are they feeling? Where are they? I was trying to perceive it like a comedian on the stage. When you’ve been onstage for a pretty long time—and nine years is a healthy run—and they’re still screaming, if you can get off, they’ll scream even louder. They’ll never forget you.” Whether he directly connected it in his mind or not, deciding to end the show on top may just have been the key to locking-in its immortal status with audiences—and thus its immortal profits with syndication buyers.

Seinfeld accomplished his goal. Audiences—and syndicators—indeed have never forgotten “Seinfeld.” As anticipated, the show’s second syndication cycle in 1998 was a bonanza. Revenue reportedly doubled to over $1 billion, a figure all the more stunning given the show would no longer benefit from NBC’s “Must See TV” marketing juggernaut.

Thanks to the immense distribution from this record syndication cycle (and those that followed), Seinfeld became a ubiquitous presence on American television sets over the subsequent years (and, later, on PCs, phones and tablets). The show’s continued presence in our minds and living rooms has become almost a self-fulfilling prophecy: you watch it because it’s familiar. “Yada yada yada,” “shrinkage,” and “double dipping” are all so burned into our psyches that they have entered the modern lexicon and stayed there.

The show that began as edgy and daring has become a sort of collective American comfort food. And that’s a valuable dish to serve—“Seinfeld” survived TV’s transition to streaming shockingly well. While the economics of new production shows have nosedived with the collapse of the broadcast monoculture, “Seinfeld” rights have remained one of highest dollar-fetching properties of the streaming era. Netflix reportedly paid well over $500 million for 5-year streaming rights to the show in 2019, and recently renewed the deal for a similar amount.

So, we return to the business logic of Jerry turning down Jack’s offer. In total, we estimate that “Seinfeld” has fetched over $4.5 billion in syndication and home video revenue since it went off the air 28 years ago—which by our conservative napkin math puts Seinfeld’s back-end share north of $600 million. The bulk of that revenue has come in later years—continued strong demand in syndication cycles three, four, and beyond into streaming. Which makes Seinfeld’s “artistically correct” comedic instinct to go out on top at least five times more valuable in the long run than Welch’s then-massive offer.

In that vein, Seinfeld’s response to HBR’s 2017 suggestion that McKinsey could have helped him extend the show contained more business wisdom than he knew. Was McKinsey funny? No. “Then,” he replied, “I don’t need them.”
