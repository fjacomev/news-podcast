Energy prices matter way more for inflation than they used to: Fed report

2026-11-11

Por Hamza Shaban

https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/article/energy-prices-matter-way-more-for-inflation-than-they-used-to-fed-report-110000366.html

Since the start of the Iran war, investors have been wrestling with what elevated oil prices mean for the economy.

Trump and his economic advisers advanced the traditional view that price increases from an oil supply shock would be short-lived, rather than a persistent threat to stability.

But many agitated consumers — and economists — saw higher gas prices and more expensive goods as a new, stubborn feature of everyday spending, a consequence of the conflict permeating deeper into the economy.

A new research note from the Federal Reserve offers a fresh take on the energy price debate. The "transitory" or temporary shocks are adding up to make a meaningful — and unpleasant — difference.

Inflation in the post-pandemic economy sticks around for longer, the report found, and oil shocks more strongly leak into core inflation, presenting a challenge for central bankers. Where in a previous era the Fed might have been correct to "look through" an oil spike as a fleeting event, the new research suggests energy shocks matter a lot more now.

Which makes sense: Between the post-COVID inflation boom, Russia-Ukraine, and Iran/Hormuz, it's been, as many analysts have punned, "one supply shock after another."

"Our estimated model implies that the recent rise in oil prices could significantly raise core inflation in the advanced economies over the next few years were the greater persistence and higher pass-through to continue," the Fed's report said.

Before the pandemic and subsequent crises, central bankers could more easily manage oil-price shocks. Inflation didn't persist as long, so any increases faded quickly. That helps explain the belief that the closure of the Strait of Hormuz (assuming the war would end) wouldn't amount to a dramatic and extended economic event. Oil price increases also tended to stay within headline inflation metrics. That meant gas and energy costs would rise, but those pricing pressures would stay confined and woudn't spread into core inflation.

Those dynamics gave central bankers, political leaders, and consumers protections from geopolitical instability.

A conflict over energy might create an economic ripple that would soon subside. But the post-pandemic data, the Fed's research shows, points to a shift in the inflation process. If it continues, it means the economy is more sensitive to oil price shocks. And higher energy prices could easily bleed into core inflation.

The Fed offered a note of caution, however: This is still a very short period, post-pandemic. This could be the new normal, or it could be a weird anomaly.

"Nevertheless," the report said, "they illustrate an upside risk to inflation should oil prices remain elevated or supply-chain disruptions persist for an extended period."
