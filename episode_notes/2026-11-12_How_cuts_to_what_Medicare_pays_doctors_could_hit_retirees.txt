How cuts to what Medicare pays doctors could hit retirees

One of the big selling points of traditional Medicare is there are no restrictions on which doctors you can see for your care.

Medicare Advantage plans, the alternative to traditional Medicare offered through private companies, on the other hand, often have a limited network of providers that are ever-changing.

But newly announced cuts to the rates Medicare pays doctors could pose a problem for patients no matter which program they're in.

Last week, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) announced cuts to Medicare payments to physicians for 2027. In 2027, Medicare will trim 2.3% off the baseline rate used to calculate payments to doctors.

"Any cut to primary care payments puts practices at risk, worsens physician shortages, and could make it harder for Medicare patients to see their doctor when they need to," Kisha Davis, president of the American Academy of Family Physicians, told Yahoo Finance.

Under the changes, when an office visit and minor procedure occur on the same day, CMS will reduce payment by 20% for the lower-cost service. The most expensive service, for example, a surgical procedure, would be paid at 100% and all other procedures on the same day would be paid at 80%.

Physicians are not required to participate in Medicare, though the majority of them do.

Medicare payments to physicians are already lower, on average, than payments from private insurers and are not automatically indexed to keep pace with inflation in medical practice costs, according to KFF.

Medicare physician payments fell 33% from 2001 to 2025 when adjusted for inflation, even as practice expenses keep climbing, according to an analysis by the American Medical Association.

The Medicare Economic Index (MEI), which tracks practice cost inflation, jumped 60% during that same period, according to the AMA.

"Imagine going 20 years without a raise while the cost of running your practice, keeping your staff and buying equipment keeps rising," Davis said. "For some practices, a further cut could mean closing their doors, and for patients, longer waits for appointments and fewer options for care close to home."

The impact doesn't stop at Medicare, she added. Tricare, state Medicaid agencies, and many other insurers base their payments on the Medicare physician fee schedule.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. said in a statement. "We are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste … changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

For now, seniors don't need to panic that their doctor is suddenly going to cancel appointments because of the scaled-back payment schedule. But the payment squeeze does have potential ramifications for some patients if their favorite physician chooses to limit the number of Medicare patients they accept. It's impossible to predict that at this stage.

This issue has been brewing, as physician groups and some policymakers have raised concerns that physicians would opt out of Medicare due to reductions in payments, potentially leading to a shortage of physicians willing to treat people with Medicare.

"On behalf of all seniors who rely on the program for their health and well-being, we want to see a high percentage of providers continue to participate in traditional Medicare," said Max Richtman, president and CEO of the National Committee to Preserve Social Security and Medicare.

While the overall number of doctors who choose not to take on Medicare patients is low, opt-out rates are somewhat higher for certain specialties, such as psychiatry and plastic and reconstructive surgery.

"While provider payment can certainly lead to provider choices about whether or not to participate in the Medicare program, virtually all non-pediatric physicians participate, so this change in payment isn't likely to have a major impact on access to physicians in Medicare," David Lipschutz, co-director of the Center for Medicare Advocacy, told Yahoo Finance.
