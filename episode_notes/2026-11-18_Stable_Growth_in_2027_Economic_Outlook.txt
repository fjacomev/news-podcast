Stable Growth in 2027 Economic Outlook

Key Takeaways

AI infrastructure spending, led by the U.S., should keep global growth steady through 2028, even as energy prices and central bank tightening weigh on some regions.

Morgan Stanley Research expects U.S. AI investment to help global growth hold at 3.3% in 2027 and 3.4% in 2028, down slightly from an estimated 3.5% in 2026.

Rate hikes by developed market central banks should peak in early 2027, and inflation should ease gradually if oil prices stay contained, though core inflation could prove stickier.

Risks run both ways: Even faster growth could result from greater demand momentum or more productivity gains. A shortfall in AI spending or another spike in energy prices would have downside implications for growth.

Solid global economic growth likely will continue in the coming year, helped by capital expenditures for the artificial intelligence buildout and strength in the U.S.

Morgan Stanley Research forecasts the global economy will grow 3.3% in 2027 and 3.4% in 2028, down slightly from an estimated 3.5% in 2026. With developed market central banks hiking interest rates to fight inflation, growth may soften modestly at the start of 2027 and then recover.

“We’re constructive on economic growth in the U.S. and globally,” says Seth Carpenter, Morgan Stanley’s Chief Global Economist and Head of Macro Strategy. Still, the inflation path has become murky. “The energy price shock has reignited headline inflation and raised the prospect of second-round inflation across the globe.”

AI Spending Fuels Strong U.S. Growth

AI infrastructure investment underpins strong U.S. growth, a key driver of the global outlook. Morgan Stanley equity analysts continue to expect hyperscaler capex will climb to about $1.6 trillion in 2027 and almost $1.8 trillion in 2028.

AI-related capital spending and productivity growth should add more than 1 percentage point to U.S. GDP growth as the economy expands by a forecast 2.5% in 2027 and 2.7% in 2028.

“The AI-driven capital expenditures and consumer spending fueled by high-income households are going to keep U.S. growth robust next year,” Carpenter explains. “That strength will help insulate the rest of the world from downside risks.”

Europe, Asia Hold Up as Emerging Markets Slow

Asia’s broader capex super-cycle remains strong, allowing the region to weather higher energy prices and rates. Europe should also see sustained solid growth, with Germany benefiting from further fiscal support, while France lags amid budget constraints. Softer growth at the start of the year will likely be most visible in Japan and the UK, as energy costs weaken demand.

In emerging markets, Morgan Stanley economists see slowing growth. Energy costs are driving stronger inflationary pressures in these economies, prompting policy responses that lean more toward restricting growth. China is an exception, with export-driven growth and subdued inflation.

Rate Hikes Should Be Short-Lived

Developed market central banks have raised rates to contain the inflation set off by the energy price shock. From here, the current interest-rate hiking cycle should be relatively short-lived.

Morgan Stanley Research economists expect the Federal Reserve to raise the U.S. benchmark rate to a peak of 4.25%–4.50% in the first quarter of 2027, up from the current 3.75%–4.00%. They see the European Central Bank hiking rates to 3.0%, up from 2.5% today, and the Bank of Japan to 1.75%, up from 1.25%. The Bank of England is also likely to hike. They expect a modest rate cutting cycle in 2028.

“Even central banks that are not hiking are keeping policy tighter than might otherwise have been expected,” Carpenter says.

Inflation Eases Gradually, But Pressures Linger

Globally, Morgan Stanley Research thinks inflation will ease gradually in 2027, provided oil prices don’t spike again. The disinflation trend should take hold in the second half of next year and begin to approach inflation targets of developed-market central banks in 2028.

Morgan Stanley economists see global core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy, slowing gradually from 2.5% in 2026 to 2.4% in 2027 and 2.2% in 2028. In the U.S., the core measure declines from 3.2% in 2026 to 2.6% in 2027 and 2.2% in 2028 amid resilient demand and gradual productivity improvement.

With oil prices expected to move lower, headline inflation should also come down globally, but lingering second-round pressures could make it harder for central banks to bring core inflation back to targets in 2027.

Diesel prices are likely to stay high into mid-2027, even as crude moves lower, which keeps pressure on supply chains and slows the decline in core inflation. Industries from chemicals to plastics are likely to feel it. Many had delayed the impact by using stockpiled inventory or contracts that locked in lower prices, but those cushions are now becoming less effective.

“Europe, which imports much of its diesel, feels this the most,” says Rajeev Sibal, Morgan Stanley Research’s Senior Global Economist. “Asia, with the largest refining buffer, feels it least.”

What Could Change the Outlook

Morgan Stanley Research sees risks on both sides of its forecast.

“Our expectation of a brief Fed hiking cycle requires energy pressures to ease before they become more firmly embedded in expectations, and potentially in core inflation,” Sibal says.

An energy price spike from an escalating conflict in the Gulf could force central banks to tighten further and tip Europe and the U.S. toward recession. On the upside, stronger demand could lift growth but keep inflation just over 3%, forcing more hikes, while larger and faster AI productivity gains could boost growth while disinflation continues. If AI spending falls short of expectations, the correction could weigh on growth and asset prices worldwide.
