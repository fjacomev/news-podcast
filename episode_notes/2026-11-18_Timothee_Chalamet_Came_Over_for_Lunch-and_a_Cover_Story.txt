Timothee Chalamet Came Over for Lunch-and a Cover Story

2026-11-18

Por Texto propio

Vanity Fair editor Mark Guiducci welcomes the actor into his home—and readers to the 2026 Holiday Issue.

BY MARK GUIDUCCI

PHOTOGRAPHS BY AIDAN ZAMIRI

NOVEMBER 17, 2026

The history of actors appearing in *Vanity Fair* goes back about as far as the movies themselves. Making a studio-orchestrated, Edward Steichen—captured debut in the pages of this magazine was a milestone in the careers of *Gary Cooper*, *Gloria Swanson*, *Anna May Wong*, and dozens of others, many of whom never made it to Technicolor.

On more than one occasion, the idea of *Timothée Chalamet* as the *last* male movie star has been a debate here at the office, usually ending in uneasy agreement: He is one of very few left, maybe the only one of his generation. He is in the tradition of *Marlon Brando* and *Leonardo DiCaprio* in that his knowability runs inverse to his fame. Like other endangered species, his extreme rarity makes him celebrated and isolated in equal measure. And with each of these stars, one rule usually holds true: The bigger they get, the less they open their mouth.

All of which is why we are thrilled to have Chalamet on our cover as he sets out to open the final, breathtaking installment of *Denis Villeneuve's Dune trilogy*. He and our team, led by photographer Aidan Zamiri, fashion editor Taylor McNeill (with whom I was an assistant at this magazine in 2010), and *VF* global creative director Jennifer Pastore, trekked to Iceland for a multiday shoot to capture his cover story “*A Star on Planet Earth*. ” Everyone involved—including and especially Timothée—approached it more like a film, or maybe a music video, than a typical cover, and there is a spiritual quality that runs throughout the pictures. “I love that being on the edge of a cliff in Iceland,” Timothée tells me, “you get the same feeling as being in Grand Central.”

Timothée also had the idea that I write the cover story myself, and while I was reluctant, I eventually thought, Why not mix it up? He came over to my apartment in early October for a conversation about *Dune*; his career; the careers of other actors, musicians, and painters; and especially the careers of the *New York Knicks*. I took away from our meeting that he may be a movie star, sure, but he approaches everything as an artist. The writing process is a humbling one, though—okay, it's agony—and since filing the cover story, I have come to believe that every editor really should write a feature now and again to make us more understanding of our writers. Perhaps once a year. Consider it a new rule at *Vanity Fair*.

Timothée and I were both reading Kevin Roose's *The AGI Chronicles* when we met up, and he pointed out that the first several chapters of the book might as well have been the source material for *Artificial*, *Luca Guadagnino's new film starring Andrew Garfield* and Yura Borisov. The film presents a villain origin story of OpenAI's cofounders, particularly *CEO Sam Altman*. It is the natural inheritor to David Fincher and Aaron Sorkin's 2010 film, *The Social Network*, which similarly helped answer the how-did-we-get-here question for another quite recent era of technology.

In case you missed it, Altman was recently on the cover of *Vanity Fair*, sitting atop our revived New Establishment list. It inspired a torrent of furious reader feedback, including a few death threats. I understand that those messages were rooted in genuine fear of this technology and ire toward its Dr. Frankenstein founders (and me, by extension, for covering them). But reporting on the powerful—whether power is granted by elections or equities or zettaflops of compute—has always been the mandate of this magazine and will continue to be. We'll keep the movie stars too.

COVER STORY

A Star on Planet Earth

From the deserts of Arrakis to the lonely fjords of Iceland, Timothée Chalamet doesn't do anything halfway. Mark Guiducci invites a celebrity of celestial proportions into his home for a chat about the Knicks, *Dune*, and everything after.

BY MARK GUIDUCCI

PHOTOGRAPHS BY AIDAN ZAMIRI

NOVEMBER 17, 2026

Shoes on or off?" Timothée Chalamet asks, like any good New Yorker entering someone's apartment. On is just fine.

He's carrying a white paper deli bag—"starving," he says—so I set him up at the dining table, where he unfurls a Lenwich Italiano. He devours it in less than two minutes. We've met before, but definitely never hung out at home. I think he's friends with Frank Ocean, so I throw on *Channel Orange*. I had stocked up on a variety of beer and tea, can offer a coffee or a cocktail, but Chalamet wants New York City tap water, please, no ice.

I live in a one-bedroom in the East 60s, the second floor of an 1870 town house. The walls are white, the floors are black, and there are flowers from the bodega on the coffee table.

Chalamet is in earth-toned gorpcore, from the sand-colored Gnuhr puffer all the way down to a tawny pair of Roas, and would look equally at home camouflaged on the streets of Manhattan or trekking in the Dolomites, which he has recently done with his father. Both ears are pierced. Except for a navy blue novelty tourist cap which reads, in a comically fancy font, “Hvar, Croatia”—another recent vacation destination, with *girlfriend Kylie Jenner*— Chalamet’s color palette is pure Arrakis.

He's just come from the Fiorello H. LaGuardia High School of Music & Art and Performing Arts, his alma mater located directly across the park, where he spoke with a group of drama students. Tonight he's going to a Yankees game with his mom, the former New York City Ballet prima ballerina, and his sister, Pauline.

Late lunch concluded, he relocates to the sofa. “Ahhh,” he says, taking a big seat and resting his right arm along the back.

He glances down at the old issues of *Vanity Fair* on my coffee table and comes to a stop on one in particular: June 1995. *Courtney Love is on the cover*, photographed by Herb Ritts, wearing massive white-feathered wings in homage to the *Nike of Samothrace* at the Louvre. Inside, there's a Ritts portrait of a toddler Frances Bean.

"This wasn't the crazy one," I say, guessing at what he's thinking.

Three years prior to this cover, in the *September 1992* issue of *Vanity Fair*, Lynn Hirschberg profiled Love and quoted her saying that she used heroin before and at the beginning of her pregnancy. The story sparked a crisis for the young Cobain family that resulted in an investigation by the department of children and family services and Cobain threatening Hirschberg and her dog. In the annals of celebrity profiles, it goes down as one of the most scalding of all time.

Chalamet knows all about that story because he's seen *Montage of Heck*, the Cobain documentary that features the *Vanity Fair* article. "It was mind-blowing to me because Lynn has only ever been to me just a figure of warmth..." Chalamet says, trailing off.

He puts the magazine back slowly, like it might burn him.

I'll be honest with you: That's not what's about to happen here today. I'm not that scary, and he's not using heroin. Nevertheless, this guy's jumpy.

It's dangerous business for a movie star to open his mouth these days. Chalamet knows that better than anyone. At 30, he's been famous his entire adult life. He's dating one of the most visible women in the world. And he is acutely aware how a single comment in *Vanity Fair* could ricochet around the pinball machine of the internet. Earlier this year, during a no-holds-barred Oscar campaign for *Marty Supreme*, an offhand remark comparing the film industry to ballet and opera was met with outsize outrage, as some saw an opportunity to take Hollywood's boy wonder down a peg. Those aware that he's from a performing arts family knew what he meant. ("Is he wrong?" *New York Times'* dance critic, Gia Kourlas, asked.) He doesn't want to revisit that with me, perhaps because as he enters another awards season, he's cautious in a way that only comes with experience.

In our 90 minutes together, Chalamet is both earnest and also reluctant to be profiled. He is by turns generous and guarded, confident and careful. He prefaces answers to no fewer than seven different questions by saying that he doesn't want to sound "pretentious." Truthfully, he never does. He's protective of his personal life and will not discuss his girlfriend, nor much outside of filmmaking. "I wish I didn't have to talk too much," Chalamet says. And yet here he is, all across Iceland in our cover shoot, and now on my blue velvet sofa.

Like every conversation in late 2026, ours starts with AI. "Thou shall not make a machine in the likeness of a man's mind," Chalamet reminds me, is one of the opening lines of *Dune*, Frank Herbert's seminal 1965 sci-fi novel. The story takes place in a world where computers are banned long after an AI apocalypse like the ones that are presaged by current-day tech CEOs and causing so much societal agita.

Chalamet tells me he's reading *The AGI Chronicles*, tech journalist Kevin Roosevelt's new book that maps out the sprint to reach artificial general intelligence. He points out that the first four chapters of the book track the plot of *Artificial*, referring to Luca Guadagnino's new Sam Altman biopic. "This is five seconds ago." He recommended the book to *Marty Supreme* director Josh Safdie, who later tells me Chalamet "found it to be not only informative, it's helped with anxiety. The more you know, the less anxious you are about it."

Just when everyone seems at least a little bit anxious about the end of the human species, along comes *Dune's* computer-free vision of the future that is, as Chalamet puts it, “strangely superhuman, strangely very human.”

You don't need to have seen the conclusion of Denis Villeneuve's trilogy to understand a few things about it. First of all, that it is enormous. Its scale is even bigger than *The Odyssey*'s. The grand vision of Villeneuve's intergalactic war drama actually makes the adaptation of the West's first epic poem (and presumed best picture front-runner so far) look like an exercise in restraint.

The production design of *Dune: Part Three*, set 20,000 years in the future, is largely inspired by the pangeographical past. There are aesthetic references as far-ranging as the samurai and SEAL Team 6, the Roman Empire by way of the Third Reich, Islamic extremism and the Russian Orthodox Church, the Bedouins and the Asaro mud men of Papua New Guinea, and perhaps more than any other single culture, ancient Egypt. And like the pyramids themselves, the visual effect of the film is mystical rather than technological, even though both obviously are technical achievements. (No AI was used in the making of *Dune: Part Three*.)

Chalamet gives Villeneuve all the credit for what he calls the “titanic” scope of the film, constructed on the foundation of the first two over the course of a decade. “It seems like he just kept building and becoming more ferociously in control of it,” he says. “Much like how Paul gets good at riding the sandworm, Denis took hold of these films.” The movie makes it easy to see how *Star Wars*—and particularly Anakin Skywalker’s character arc—is often considered derivative of *Dune*. (For the real heads: The story of *Dune: Part Three* is roughly equivalent to *Star Wars* episodes IV through VI, all in one film.)

"It's huge, huge geography," Chalamet says. "Do you think you could have done this when you were in your 30s?" Chalamet asked Villeneuve on set. "He goes, 'No way.' " The director went on to tell him that he passed on a "huge sci-fi project" earlier in life that he wouldn't even reveal to Chalamet, but Villeneuve "turned it down precisely because he felt like he didn't have the tools yet."

Production designer Patrice Vermette's life-size sets were so imposing that Chalamet spent time on location in Budapest ahead of the film's shoot, immersing himself in the palace of Arrakis so that he wouldn't be distracted by its grandeur when the cameras rolled. "The whole thing is daunting," Chalamet says. "I didn't want any part of the day to be lost to 'Wow, look at what I'm stepping into,'" he adds later. Compared to shooting the first two *Dune* films, he was more or less alone on set. Chalamet remembers it as "a more claustrophobic, isolated experience." The way Villeneuve puts it: "Timothée created a bubble around himself which allowed him to delve more deeply into the depressive darkness of his character." He was, after all, preparing to play a murderer.

*Part Three* encounters Chalamet's antihero, Duke Paul Atreides (also known by his Fremen alias Usul, as the religious cult figure Muad'Dib, and the Padishah Emperor of the known universe—you got all that?), at the height of his totalitarianism and the nadir of his soul. Sixty-one billion is the number of deaths attributable to Atreides, who in the Herbert novels compares himself to Adolf Hitler and Genghis Khan. He is a “psychopath,” according to Florence Pugh's Princess Irulan. Villeneuve tells me there was another detached paranoid figure he compared Atreides to: Howard Hughes.

To get into character, Chalamet studied the personalities of world leaders— “everyone good and bad” and particularly “megalomaniacs across human history.” He notes that “Frank Herbert wrote *Messiah* to course correct...the first *Dune*, where Paul is sort of revered as a hero.” He continues: “This is a cautionary tale against cults of personality and figures of charisma.” When I ask if Donald Trump was on his mind, he says “not particularly,” noting that power and its corrosive effects on the soul are “eternally prescient themes.” The film is ultimately a story of atonement—if not redemption—for a fallen messiah.

It was all this research that compelled Chalamet to shave off his globally

recognized curly mop. “Decision fatigue is a real thing amongst the world

leaders, good and bad, current and past.... The idea of not having to change

clothes, not having to sweep the hair out of your face, simple shit like that.”

And so Chalamet said goodbye to his Samson-like locks. Did he feel...“like

Anne Hathaway in *Les Mis?*” Exactly. Or Natalie Portman in *V for Vendetta*, I offer. (He loves *V for Vendetta*.) Perhaps Demi Moore in *G.I. Jane*. “We would [shave] it basically every other day. My hair grows quickly, so we would get after it.” Today the curls, as you can see, have returned to their natural state. Power restored.

"Timothée has become much more ambitious as an actor," Villeneuve tells me when I ask how he's changed in the decade that they've worked together. "He is exploring new artistic paths in a highly exciting way." Costar Javier Bardem says, "In a way, his growth has been almost chronological with that of his character. In *Dune: Part Three*, there is a real gravitas to him. He stands firmly in his place, with a confidence and authority that he has earned through his work, his sacrifices, his dedication, and his commitment as an actor and as a professional. You can see it and feel it, and it is incredibly reaffirming to witness."

Safdie puts it like this: “I believe he’s like hi-fi sound. A good hi-fi system you can listen to in a tiny room and it makes the room feel huge. And he has that ability as an actor...his style of acting is larger-than-life realism.”

“He is always restlessly searching to keep the scene alive,” says Greta Gerwig, who directed Chalamet in *Lady Bird* and *Little Women*. “He needs room to scream a scene or whisper it or jump up onto a table or pirouette.”

When I ask Chalamet if being famous his entire adult life prepared him to play a lonely-at-the-top emperor, he points to the moment in the first *Dune* when Paul and his mother, Lady Jessica, arrive on Arrakis and are greeted by the entire awe-inspiring army. “There were moments” in his own life, he says, when he realized about his relationship with the public: “Oh, something has shifted.”

Wandering around my apartment, Chalamet is observant and interested. “The picture in your bathroom looks like Greta.” He means Gerwig, though it’s actually Edie Sedgwick balancing on one leg in arabesque, cigarette in hand. (Sedgwick lived in this apartment during her Warhol years, and the picture was taken in the living room where we are sitting.) We look through the 1965 issue of *Vogue*, in which the picture originally ran.

He points out the French poster for *The Graduate* in the bedroom, in which a pink Matisse-like cutout of a woman's elegant leg hovers in a thought bubble above Dustin Hoffman's head. "I didn't know it was called that," he says. "*Le Lauréat.*" (One journalist recommended that I speak French with Chalamet, as they find him more open when they do. Sadly, I can hardly order dinner in French.)

He loves an Annie Leibovitz picture of Cindy Sherman made up as the sad clown in Edward Hopper's 1914 *Soir Bleu* and points out monographs about Ed Ruscha and Harmony Korine on my bookshelf. ("Beast!" he declares about Korine.)

He sweetly asks me about a picture of me officiating my best friends' wedding.

We're back on the sofa now. I'd asked Chalamet to do a little homework before our meeting: to come prepared to talk about his favorite movie performances. He was a diligent student, but instead came with four films and one play that specifically helped him get ready for his *Vanity Fair* cover shoot.

"Tree of Life," he begins, citing Terrence Malick's impressionistic masterpiece as the "biggest inspiration" for the cover shoot, photographed by Aidan Zamiri over three days in Iceland. "It could have been accomplished over a half day in the studio," Chalamet says, but they traveled thousands of miles because, as in *Tree of Life*, "the process is almost as important as the finished product."

Next up is *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*. "It's a kick-ass performance, and it's provocative and heartwarming and full of life and one of the great movie performances of all time." He cites *Cloud Atlas*, which the Wachowski sisters made years after *The Matrix* trilogy, as achieving the kind of scale that inspired the team to trek to the edge of Icelandic cliffs and inside waterfalls. (It's also one of two films, along with Bruce Robinson's 1987 *Withnail and I*, that Christopher Nolan has ever recommended to him.)

Chalamet whips out his orange iPhone—where he has incidentally started a new daily habit of playing chess—to show me the trailer for the Francis Ford Coppola-produced 1982 art house film *Koyaanisqatsi*, a hypnotic succession of increasingly uneasy urban vignettes, sped up and slowed down. “It’s about the humility you must feel as any person within the scale of the universe,” Chalamet says. “I love that being on the edge of a cliff in Iceland, you get the same feeling as being in Grand Central.” This one was recommended by Safdie and is clearly an inspiration for the relentless, kinetic pacing of his own movies as well. Chalamet is so close to Safdie that even the chess habit is inspired by the match that Safdie and Alfonso Cuarón continue in perpetuity.

"What's interesting about Timmy is he's incredibly curious and very sincere, but also has a pretty sharp sense of humor and can be quite jagged," Safdie himself tells me a week later. "There's a goofy seriousness to him. He likes to be provoked."

And finally, he gives me *Man to Man*, Tilda Swinton's one-woman show currently on at London's Royal Court Theatre. "It's about the incredible futility of existence and a person losing their mind," Chalamet says. "I liked it in this little program I created."

I've never met any actor who prepared for a photo shoot the way he would for a role in a movie. I get the sense that's just Chalamet's way: Everything he does in public is considered part of the art. Everything except, perhaps, courtside appearances.

Little provokes Chalamet more than the New York Knicks, and it is the subject about which he gets most animated. “It’s the most quintessential New York team” for many reasons, he says, but chief among them is location, location, location. “It’s not like the Yankees or Mets, Giants, or Jets. It’s in the heart of Manhattan, and it reflected the agony of the New York experience for decades.” Chalamet says he lives two lives: one as himself and one as “fan with a capital F.”

When I ask what his greatest extravagance is, the immediate and definite answer is “sports tickets.” “Those away games weren’t cheap, but...it is the most fun I’ve had in my life as a human.” We know this. Because the best word to describe pictures of Chalamet courtside and in the Knicks locker room is simply *ecstatic*.

He tells me about traveling through the night from San Antonio back to New York after the Game 5 championship as an all-time life high. “I flew with people that had been working at the Garden for decades,” he says, explaining that Ben Stiller and Fat Joe were along for the ride on an MSG-owned plane. (“I don’t want to sound too out of touch,” he worries. I assure him the reader will understand that commercial airlines aren’t running 2 a.m. departures.) “It was just incredibly raucous, everyone celebrating.”

Chalamet says he will always remember Jalen Brunson's quiet leadership as the hallmark of the season overall. "It was not the cult of personality" you might expect to lead a championship team, he notes. "It was someone actually quite soft-spoken, reserved, and hardworking, and unassuming."

On topics other than basketball, he is less enthusiastic. "My work is my bones and muscles. I have little interest in putting more of myself out there," he says. What's something that no one asks you that you wish they would? "Nothing. I mean, nothing. I'm not being passive-aggressive about this interview at all, but I think..." he trails off. Chalamet points at the speaker, which is still playing Frank Ocean. "He's one of the rare people where the vacuum of what's out there about him helps drive the..." "Curiosity?" I offer. "Beyond curiosity, how brilliant his artistry is. But the economy of movies," Chalamet says, thinking. "You got to sell a lot of tickets."

Which is exactly the reason that we're sitting here together now. Who is his dream collaborator, living or dead? After a long silence, I tell him he can pass. "Okay. Pass." What does he think of *David Ellison's Skydance* taking over Warner Bros., the studio behind *Dune*? "I'm going to hit a Frank Ocean and be mysterious," is all he says.

He's vague about future projects. "I got one thing with a phenomenal filmmaker," he says. "It's kind of a no-brainer project, and should it get announced, I think you'll see what I mean."

Would he ever return to the character of Elio, the protagonist of *Call Me by Your Name* that gave him his star turn? "A great question. Never say never, but...I've grown up with the mindset that you don't really go near something that is so near and dear to people." So, that's a no. "Now, to work with Luca again? In a heartbeat."

Is there any actor, or artist of any kind, after whose career he would like to model his own? “Who was [Bob] Dylan looking at? Dylan.” He broke the mold, I offer. “He created the mold.” Chalamet wants to be an original too (and perhaps this is what he meant when he declared that he was in “pursuit of greatness” at the 2025 SAG Awards). “It’s less sexy to some degree to wade out in the unknown, but it’s more authentic,” he says. “Especially in the movie industry where people could feel like, *Oh, that was the golden age....* You gotta cook it up in a unique way.”

Around 6 p.m., Chalamet gets up off the couch and suits back up in his gorpcore. The Yankees game starts in just a couple of hours, and he has to shower and collect his mom and sister and make his way to the Bronx. I walk him downstairs, amble down the stoop, and almost want to say: Look! We survived!

Now that wasn't so bad, was it?
