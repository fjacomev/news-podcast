A diversified investment strategy is not just prudent – it is essential

By Elias Nechachby

A year ago investors were bracing themselves for a year of uncertainty, upheaval and market volatility driven by conflicts, elections and other economic challenges around the world. Twelve months later, as we look to 2025, the picture is even more challenging.

While global rallies have benefited the equity markets around the world, valuations are now richer, and risks are bubbling under the surface.

Investors interested in shoring up their portfolios are likely attentive to the increased geopolitical risks following the new US presidency, and escalations of war across Russia and the Middle East.

The VIX Index, one of the most popular measures of expected stock market volatility, has had several big spikes in 2024 and is expected to continue to surprise investors in 2025. So, finding a way to manage expected market volatility will be critical for securing robust returns – and peace of mind.

Geopolitical, trade and economic risks

Looking forward, the febrile geopolitical, trade and economic backdrop points to greater volatility. And with some stock valuations over-extended, any upset could lead to a sizeable correction and in the worst case to a hard landing.

For example, the prospect of the US imposing import tariffs on goods from Canada, Mexico and China – its three biggest trading partners apart from the EU – would have ramifications for global businesses and their supply chains. It would also likely have inflationary, currency and other economic repercussions, all of which could have other unforeseen and unwelcome consequences.

So, finding a way to manage expected market volatility will be critical for securing robust returns – and peace of mind.

Confronted with that challenge, investors need to look carefully at their investment strategy to try and insulate their portfolio – after all, robustness is not achieved by chance.

Engineering investment learnings

Diversification is more often than not depicted as the diversification of assets, countries, sectors or stocks to insulate your portfolio against risk.

However, this type of diversification is insufficient as it only works in mild local market corrections, and does not work during global crises such as the 2008 global financial crisis (GFC) or 2020 Covid-19 pandemic.

As a starting point, to insulate their portfolio against potential risks, even though they may not be apparent in the short to medium term, investors need to look beyond the classic asset classes, sector, and country diversification, which notoriously fail to provide investors with any kind of protection during a severe crisis, when all correlations jump to one.

The traditional 60/40 stocks and bonds investment strategy saw a disastrous 2022, declining 17 per cent, with both asset classes seeing downward swings.

As an ex-aeronautical engineer, I approach risk in terms of ‘independent redundancy’. What I mean here is that an investment strategy needs to have several ‘engines’ that allow it to navigate different types of markets, and generate performance whether the market is up, down or flat.

Each ‘engine’ should be truly independent, or else there is an increased risk that they will all fail to perform at the same time. In aeronautics, you have to prove that the likelihood of undetected error for any critical system (that is, ‘the integrity’) is less than one in a billion. To reduce the probability of error and to reach that figure, one must have enough built-in independent redundancies.

That is why investors should look to diversify sources of alpha. Spreading investments across independent performance engines reduces exposure to concentrated risks, and benefit from varying growth opportunities and market dynamics, particularly as US trade policies, tax regulations, and global geopolitical shifts add uncertainty.

A diversified strategy with orthogonal components/sources of alpha can better adapt to changing market dynamics.

Orthogonal sources of alpha can provide stability

Orthogonal sources of alpha mean some of these strategies performed very strongly in 2008, March 2020 and in 2022, and did not destroy wealth outside these crises while others did well in better market conditions.

This multi-layered approach ensures that an investor’s financial strategy remains robust in unpredictable markets.

Markets very often form consensus views about topics such as interest rate changes, economic growth, regulatory shifts and wars, and this is what the markets price in.

Being more knowledgeable about what the consensus is helps investors better identify the risks that are on the other end of the consensus, ie the contrarian scenarios.

For instance, the ongoing crisis in the Middle East is having quasi/no impact on the stock market recently, as it is taken for granted.

The Trump administration’s proposed policies, including tariffs on imports, tax cuts and the deregulation of industries including traditional energy sectors and cryptocurrencies, all are expected to benefit US stocks.

This means the risk is if the consensus turns out to be wrong, for example, if US debt becomes unsustainable, if inflation surges back in the US, if the US enters a recession.

These are not priced in, and these are the risks a robust strategy needs to protect against.

Monitor several risk metrics

However, using more than one measure of risk is advisable – a low volatility does not always protect you against significant drawdowns.

When most strategies are suffering, markets are crashing and leveraged beta portfolios are getting decimated, MoSAIQ can generate strong positive absolute performance, help diversify away investors’ market risks and therefore reduce overall portfolio drawdown.

Maximum drawdown (MDD) is perhaps the most useful risk metric, as it measures the maximum fall in the value an investment experiences from peak to trough over a given period or over its whole history. Past MDD contrary to past performance and past volatility has shown to carry some predictive power. This helps investors take a more informed investment decision. MDD can also be used to assess for how much an investment might drop in value during a downturn. For example, if a stock has a historical MDD of -80 per cent, investors should prepare for at least similar drops in the future. Knowing the MDD helps set realistic expectations, manage risk more pertinently during drawdowns and avoid emotional decisions.

Avoiding emotional trades

Have you ever booked a holiday during peak holiday season, seeing flight and hotel prices rise, and some time later, realised you could have had the same holiday for half the price if you had booked earlier or waited for the off-season?

Similarly, impulsive overbuying during rallies – and the opposite: panic selling during downturns – will leave you with less value for your money.

Markets will always correct themselves, and selling stocks when markets dip or overpaying when prices inflate prevents you from benefiting to the same extent as had you been patient.

When stock markets globally crashed due to the Covid-19 pandemic in late February 2020, 1.4mn retail investors each sold more than £10,000 of their shares, despite equities regaining considerable ground by the summer and tech shares surging, losing out when markets dropped and missing the following rebound.

Volatility – when changes in the price of stocks or currencies are sharp, rapid and unpredictable – is typically measured using annualised standard deviation of returns.

Under normal circumstances the VIX measure falls within a 10-20 band, averaging around 15. VIX is usually higher in the longer term, for example seven to nine months, and then levels decrease in the short term (the VIX future curve is said to be in contango), for example as of writing the December 2024 VIX Future contract is trading at 14.85, while the VIX July 2025 future contract is at 17.98

An extreme example of high volatility, which should inform all investors as they shape their portfolios, was with the VIX hitting 82.7 at the outbreak of the Covid pandemic mid-March 2020, when the average for the previous year was 15.4. Most recently, this year it jumped +181 per cent in a single day on August 5 2024, from a Friday August 2 close of 23.4 to a Monday August 5 high of 65.7 as the yen carry trade blew up.

As a starting point, volatility tends to mean reverse over time, which shows that taking a long-term approach to investment is a way to mitigate volatility risk.

This approach with regular investments allows for the benefits of compounding to kick-in. Alongside any investment, it is important not to use leverage and to have funds available for either seizing market opportunities when crises happen or for dealing with emergencies to avoid needing to sell investments at a loss simply because you need the money.

The above guidance is application to all investments and offers some protection. But there is more. Investors should look to allocate 10 per cent to 20 per cent of their portfolios to best-in-class alternative strategies that offer true diversification to preserve wealth and enhance returns.

Very often, they can provide a real hedge against a crisis that may be hurting all asset classes at the same time. For example, both stocks and commodities plunged during Covid and oil futures even traded at negative prices, while one alternative strategy was up more than plus-10 per cent in March 2020 and more than 41 per cent in calendar year 2020, moving in the opposite direction of both stocks and the energy sector.

In order to assess if a hedge fund strategy is truly uncorrelated and is bringing real diversification, investors should check the performance it had during global crises, such as the GFC, Covid-19 pandemic, and in 2022, and ask: did it perform well?

More generally, how does the strategy perform in the bottom 10 per cent of the worst stock market month? Does it have a positive average performance in these periods? Does the strategy have a lower MDD than the market?

If the answers to all the above-mentioned questions are yes, then this is a strong sign of robustness and of being best in class in the alternative world.

In a climate marked by geopolitical turmoil, economic uncertainty and rapid shifts in policy, a robust and correctly diversified investment strategy is not just prudent – it is essential.

The challenges of navigating the volatile landscape of 2025 and beyond demand a disciplined, well-informed approach to portfolio management.
