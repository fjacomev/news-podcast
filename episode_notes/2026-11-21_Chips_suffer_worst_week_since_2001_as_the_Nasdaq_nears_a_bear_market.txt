Wall Street is wrapping up a kind of week it has seen only five other times since World War II. Two of them came in 2008 and 2001.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC) fell every day this week, with four drops of 1% or more. That had happened only in 1962, 1974, 1978, the week after the 9/11 attacks in 2001 and October 2008, according to Bespoke Investment Group.

Friday brought the sharpest leg down. The S&P 500 fell nearly 4% and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC) more than 5%, wiping out the Nasdaq's gain for 2026. The S&P 500 shed about $2.4 trillion in market value on the day and closed in a correction, 10% below its Oct. 6 record, for the first time since April 2025. The Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI) closed in one too.

The Nasdaq 100 (^NDX) finished just shy of a bear market, or a 20% drop from its record closing high. Meanwhile, chip stocks had their worst week since the one after the 9/11 attacks.

The strange part is the timing. This was supposed to be a showcase week for AI. Nvidia's (NVDA) first report since Jensen Huang's exit showed revenue more than doubling, and Anthropic (ANT) pulled off the biggest IPO ever. Both stocks sank anyway. Tech stocks that beat earnings forecasts this season fell an average of 1.5% on their report days, the worst reaction to good news since at least 2001, according to Bespoke.

Nvidia has lost about $2.7 trillion in market value since its Oct. 6 top, more than the $2.5 trillion that The Kobeissi Letter called the biggest 45-day loss for any stock in history. Broadcom (AVGO), Micron (MU) and AMD (AMD) each fell about 12% to 14% Friday alone. A Yahoo Finance basket of 62 chip stocks lost about $1.5 trillion on the day, and the reasons keep piling up, including a reported SEC probe of its accounting,.

Chips are reliving 2008

The PHLX Semiconductor Index (^SOX) fell about 17% this week and has lost a third of its value since Sept. 30. That puts chips on track for their worst two months since late 2008, a pace topped only in the dot-com bust and the financial crisis. Chips hit their financial-crisis low 18 years ago to the day, on Nov. 20, 2008.

The Nasdaq 100 and the tech sector are on the same track. The S&P 500 is headed for its worst November since 1973, and the Nasdaq for its worst since 2000. The S&P 500 has given up about $8 trillion since its record, nearly $6 trillion of it this week.

That's where the 2008 comparison breaks down. In October and November 2008, the typical S&P 500 stock lost about 28%. This time, the equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP) is about flat since Sept. 30.
