Jensen Huang is back as Nvidia CEO after 12 days — Steve Jobs needed 12 years, Sam Altman less than a week

Nvidia's board needed 12 days to hit "undo" on Jensen Huang. Three years ago this week, OpenAI needed less than five to do the same with Sam Altman.

Almost nobody saw the Huang news coming. Minutes before Nvidia (NVDA) announced his return Monday morning, bettors on Polymarket gave him about a 3% chance of getting his job back this month. Five minutes later, the odds were 99%.

The board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, CEO and director, effective immediately, and Colette Kress goes back to being CFO. "The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA," Chair Harvey Jones said.

Nvidia stock closed up about 31% Monday, its best day since 2003, adding about $950 billion in market value. That's the biggest one-day gain for any US company, more than double the old record, and more than three-quarters of what Nvidia lost while Huang was gone.

Nvidia stock fell more than 4% before the bell on Tuesday.

Huang's exile was expensive

Nvidia lost about $1.2 trillion in market value from the Nov. 11 close, when the board accepted Huang's resignation, through Friday, a stretch that included the stock's worst week ever.

Silicon Valley has seen this movie before. OpenAI fired Altman on a Friday afternoon in November 2023. Microsoft (MSFT) hired him that Sunday, and more than 700 of OpenAI's roughly 770 employees threatened to follow him. By Tuesday night, he was back.

"I love OpenAI, and everything I've done over the past few days has been in service of keeping this team and its mission together," Altman wrote on X.

Huang had his own campaign, though not one he ran. A "Stand with Jensen" petition drew more than 100,000 signatures, Elon Musk's among them, including more than 5,000 from people who said they work at Nvidia. It called for "due process, not mob justice."

OpenAI's kingmaker was Microsoft. Nvidia's was the White House.

The board said input from senior levels of the US government led it to conclude that Huang poses no national security risk. President Trump had pushed for Huang to get a second chance.

Huang's undoing is different from Altman's in one way. Altman came back to a new board. Huang comes back to the same chair, plus a new national security and export control committee, a search for a chief compliance officer, and an independent investigation into reports that he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort.
